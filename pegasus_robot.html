Pegasus

Multi-function wheeled robot for 23-24 FTC Robotics Competition


As the captain and mechanical lead of my robotics team for the 23-24 season, I led the design, prototyping, manufacturing, assembling and testing of the entire robot. I also led the main components of the robot, as well as the integration as a whole.

Below are detailed highlights of each major subsystem, as well as the design and iteration processes.


Whole Robot CAD   



Entire Robot

As mechanical lead, I led the entire design of the robot, from individual components to overall cohesiveness. I took charge of leading the major component designs, and during our brainstorming session, we set three design goals for the robot: reliability, efficiency, and accuracy.

Initially, we placed our focus on individual component improvements and approximated their positions on the drivetrain. Soon, we encountered problems like poor weight distribution, clunky integration, and no space to place the battery.

Along the way, I had been meticulously documenting every part in CAD, so I was able to lead the redesign process. I optimized the drivetrain by pocketing, creating modular components, and shifting parts around for better positioning. These refinements reduced weight and increased performance, culminating in a streamlined, efficient robot.

Intake System

When designing the intake system, I wanted to optimize it to collect from the stack during autonomous and anywhere on the field during teleoperation. Over five major iterations, I carried over things that worked (compliant rollers, bottom roller) while replacing things that didn’t work (large middle roller). When trying to add a way to aid in transfer, a middle roller ended up trapping the element, so I switched to rubber noodles which provided a flicking motion that was perfect for sending it through the tray.

Inspired by a last minute save during a competition where I used a pizza plate to guide elements stuck at the entrance into the tray, we implemented a polycarbonate plate that we heat-bended. Coupled with a compact tray redesign and efficient gearboxes, the intake evolved into a reliable mechanism that efficiently collected elements.

Intake Lifting System

To lift the intake system, we initially went with a simple linkage design that lifted/lowered the intake around an axis of rotation. It was fast when switching between a downward angle to intake from the ground and an upward angle to slide the elements down into the deposition bucket. But, after shortening our intake tray, we couldn’t maintain the same point of rotation while achieving the specific angles we wanted.

So we switched to a virtual 4-bar design, with independent servos to control the lift and tilt of the intake. By programming multiple configurations, we created a highly versatile lifting system that allowed for compact storing and unique positions for each level of the stack.

Deposition System

The initial arm design was a bucket, which allowed for quick and simple depositing. Part of the game requires you to form patterns with specific color elements, but our single-latch design forced us to drop everything at once. So to have more strategic placing, I developed a dual-latch system, and depending on the slight angle shifts of the latch, you could release one at a time.

Near States Competition, we realized we wanted more accuracy. Dropping from a horizontal angle resulted in elements bouncing upon collision and landing in different positions, and the bucket walls also prevented us from dropping near the edge. To solve both of these issues, I designed an independent claw system that had two claws contoured to the shape of the elements to allow for consistent orientation when transferring and a transparent polycarbonate top for better visibility for the robot operators.

Deposition Arm

Our initial design consisted of two servos that powered the swinging motion. This was fast, but again, didn’t provide enough customizability that we wanted.

Our next design allocated one of the servos to our added wrist to rotate the claw system. A week before the State Competition, noticing the slow swinging motion, I quickly proposed a differential arm design, which doubled the torque for both motions, significantly increasing cycle speed, and allowing us to set the state record!

Sadly we didn’t keep the differential system for the World Championship, and we went with one with 3-axes of rotation with the same swinging and rotating motion, but this time with an added angular twist. This simplified autonomous paths as our robot could travel less and didn’t need to rotate in order to reach the backboard.

Differential Arm

Before States, I noticed the slow performance of the arm design and decided to implement a differential gear system. It probably wasn’t the best time to change the design, but it was a simple switch, and I was determined to make it faster.

Instead of having servos powering individual movements, the gearbox gears together both servos, allowing them to control individual movements together, hence doubling torque for both motions. Additionally, for extended reach, I mounted the system on a horizontal slide and passively powered it with a linkage.

This was a group effort, and we worked together till midnight for days to bring this design to life. Unlike my past designs, I didn’t touch CAD at all, relying only on whiteboard sketches and continuously refining until it worked. In the end, it was completely worth it, improving our States performance, and being a fun story to tell judges!

Hang System

This was the most exciting but difficult mission that year, as I had never designed anything similar to it. After my initial prototypes failed, I turned to YouTube and was inspired by an approach a FIRST Robotics Competition robot took that utilized slanted hooks and the robot’s driving momentum to slide the robot onto the bar and lift it off the ground, without the need for heavy-duty motors.

The first prototype was assembled hastily at 5 am in the morning before a competition, with slanted hooks and a counter-sprung method that allowed for an instantaneous release. This rapid prototype was refined through CAD, allowing for more compact storage within the robot, aluminum bars, and connecting both sides through a bar so that they could both be released from a single servo. This allowed us to achieve one of the fastest hang times in the world and saved us many motors and servos.
